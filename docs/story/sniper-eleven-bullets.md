# Eleven Bullets

A fifteen-part story about Sniper (Kardel Sharpeye), told in simple English. The genre is a
**western**: a lone marksman, a village in a mountain pass, an army coming up the valley, and a
countdown. Dry humour, real tension, one enormous payoff.

Each part is roughly 100 words, one part per Hero Journey level (parts 1–15 → levels 1–15). The
exception is the climax: **Part 13 and Part 14 are the legendary shot**, split across two levels,
and Part 14 is deliberately the longest part in the story.

Companion stories: `lina-golden-rolling-pin.md`, `juggernaut-sixty-one-carvings.md`,
`pudge-butcher-of-blackwater.md`.

---

## Localisation notes

**Hero names are NEVER translated.** Keep them exactly as written, in every language:

- Sniper
- Kardel Sharpeye
- Clockwerk / Rattletrap
- Snapfire / Beatrix Snapfire
- Mars

Other do-not-translate names:

- **Knollen** — the village. Leave as-is or transliterate. Do not translate the meaning.
- **Pim** — the boy. Original character, not a hero. Leave as-is.
- **Keen Folk** — Sharpeye's people. May be translated **only** if the game already has an official
  translation for it in that locale; otherwise leave as-is.
- **Hills of Druud** — same rule as Keen Folk.

Items that *should* be translated, with care:

- **"BOOM! HEADSHOT!"** — Sniper's catchphrase, shouted in Part 4 and paid off as the final line of
  Part 15. If the game has an official localised version of this voice line, use that exact
  wording, because players know it. It must stay a shout in capitals. This is the most important
  recurring line in the story.
- **The chalk numbers** — ELEVEN, TEN, NINE, SEVEN, FOUR, THREE, TWO, ONE, ZERO. Write them as
  words in capitals, the way a child would chalk them on a wall, not as digits.
- All part titles.

**Swearing — one printed swear, in Part 5.** It is the punchline of that part. Translate it with the
everyday casual equivalent in the target language, at the register of "oh crap" to "oh hell". Do not
add more anywhere else, and do not remove this one.

Tone guidance for translators: keep sentences short and words everyday. The voice is dry and
factual, and gets quieter as the story gets more serious — notice that the early parts have jokes
and the late parts almost none. Preserve that drift. Sharpeye brags constantly in the first half and
barely speaks in the second half; that change is the whole character arc. There is almost no
dialogue; keep it that way.

## Part 1 — Eleven

Among the Keen Folk, a marksman is remembered for exactly one shot. Not his best hundred. One.

Kardel Sharpeye was forty-one years old and did not have his yet. Eleven thousand good shots in his life, and not one song about any of them.

So when an army started up the valley, and somebody had to stand in the pass above Knollen, he took the job before anyone mentioned money. He said he would be famous by Tuesday. He said it twice, in case they missed it.

Then he learned that the ammunition cart had been captured two valleys over.

He had eleven bullets in his coat.

There were nine thousand men coming.

---

## Part 2 — The Wall by the Gate

A boy called Pim chalked **ELEVEN** on the stone beside the gate. Nobody asked him to.

It stayed there the whole siege, and people checked it the way you check the weather.

Knollen is two hundred Keen Folk in stone houses, wedged into the narrowest part of the pass, with a mountain leaning over the top of it.

There is no second gate. There is no road out the back. There is one way in and one way through, and whoever is standing in it is the entire plan.

Sharpeye walked the wall twice, looked down the valley, and said the view was excellent.

---

## Part 3 — Snapfire and the Bell

The village elder was a very old woman called Beatrix Snapfire. She is four feet tall. She has been shooting things for seventy years, and she was famous for it long before Sharpeye was born.

She was the only person in Knollen who did not look at him with hope, so naturally he could not stop trying to impress her.

She walked him up to the bell.

It hangs above the village and it is the size of a cart, and nobody rings it in winter. Every child in Knollen learns why before they learn to read: the snow up there sits loose, the mountain listens, and a loud enough sound will bring the whole white weight of it down into the pass.

Sharpeye said *huh*, and forgot it immediately.

---

## Part 4 — Bullet One

He told her his plan that evening. Ten bullets for the army, and the eleventh saved for the warlord himself — through the eye, in front of everybody. A song, basically, standing there waiting to happen.

Snapfire said the only shot anyone remembers is the last one, and went to bed.

The first scout came up the east track on the fourth day, nine hundred yards out. Sharpeye licked his thumb. He checked the wind twice, which he did not need to do. He announced the range out loud so everyone could confirm it later.

Then he fired, turned around with both arms in the air, and shouted **BOOM! HEADSHOT!**

Forty people watched him in complete silence.

**TEN.**

---

## Part 5 — Rattletrap

The village had a machinist. His name is Rattletrap, although outside these mountains everybody calls him Clockwerk, and Rattletrap very much prefers Clockwerk.

He announced that he would simply manufacture more bullets.

He worked nineteen hours and produced six.

The first three exploded on the bench. The fourth went backwards through his own window, and Rattletrap said *oh, fuck* loudly enough that two children repeated it at dinner. The fifth melted while sitting still, which he has never explained.

The sixth was perfect — the finest bullet ever made in the Hills of Druud. He held it up in the lamplight and the whole workshop went quiet.

Then he dropped it, and it rolled into the drain.

---

## Part 6 — Bullet Two

An army that big marches to a drum, so that every man steps at the same time. This one had a drum the size of a door, carried at the front of the column where everybody could hear it.

The wall expected him to shoot the drummer.

He shot the drum.

Nine thousand men lost the beat at the same moment. The front ranks walked into each other, the column folded up like a dropped rope, and the whole advance stopped for a day and a half while somebody rode back down the valley to fetch another one.

Snapfire looked at him slightly longer than usual that evening.

**NINE.**

---

## Part 7 — Bullets Three and Four

They came back at night with sappers, digging under the gate by lantern light, which was sensible, because a man cannot shoot what he cannot see.

He shot the lantern.

They lit a second one behind a shield and dug on, and brought up a winch to haul the loose stone out — so he put the fourth bullet through the pin of the winch. Fourteen hundred yards, in the dark, downhill, in a crossing wind.

The winch came down on top of the hole. The hole stopped being a hole.

Two bullets, one night, and not one body.

Pim chalked **SEVEN**.

---

## Part 8 — Bullets Five, Six and Seven

The ninth night was the bad one.

Three bullets in nine minutes. All three on men who were already over the outer wall and twenty feet from the gate. All three necessary. None of them clever, none of them long, none of them anything anybody would ever write down.

He did not shout afterwards. He did not announce the ranges.

He sat down on the step with his hands between his knees and could not get them to stop shaking for about an hour.

Pim chalked **FOUR**, quietly, and went away without saying anything to anybody.

---

## Part 9 — Bullet Eight

On the eleventh day Rattletrap crept out into the pass in the dark, to bury one of his machines near the enemy camp.

He was too slow. The sun came up while he was still out there in the open.

A soldier saw him and came after him with a spear. Rattletrap ran for the gate. He is sixty years old, he is wearing eighty pounds of clockwork armour, and he runs like a kettle coming to the boil.

There was fog in the pass. Sharpeye could see the soldier from the shoulders up and no further, eleven hundred yards out, moving, with Rattletrap running in the same line.

He took it anyway. The soldier went down. Rattletrap got home — still carrying the machine, which he had forgotten to leave behind.

**THREE.**

---

## Part 10 — Bullet Nine

Then they brought the ram.

It came up the pass under a roof of shields, forty men inside it, and there was nothing to shoot at. No faces, no rope, no lantern. Just a slow wooden house walking at the gate.

So he shot the pin out of the front axle.

The ram dropped, slewed sideways, and went backwards down the pass, which is steeper than it looks. It picked up speed the whole way, with its own crew first running after it and then running away from it.

It is the only time anybody on that wall laughed all week.

**TWO.**

---

## Part 11 — Bullet Ten

On the fourteenth morning the warlord came to the gate himself, under a white flag, to offer terms.

Mars stood three hundred yards out, in the open, not moving, talking.

It was the shot. The exact one he had been saving for, the one he had described out loud to anybody who would stand still since the day he arrived.

He took it.

The bullet hit the shield and went off into the sky somewhere, and Mars did not even stop speaking. He finished his terms — surrender by dawn — and walked back down the pass.

Pim chalked **ONE**, with his back to everybody.

---

## Part 12 — One

Nobody slept.

Two hundred people, nine thousand men, one bullet, and dawn coming whether anyone was ready or not. He sat up on the wall all night with the rifle across his knees and did not brag to anybody about anything.

Snapfire came and sat with him.

She told him about her own famous shot. She had been nineteen. It had been at a rope.

Nobody sings about the rope, she said. They sing about what came down when it let go.

Then she went in.

And Sharpeye, for the first time since his first morning, looked up at the mountain.

---

## Part 13 — The Last Shot

At dawn the army came into the pass. All of it. Nine thousand men packed between two walls of rock, shields up, waiting on the horn.

Sharpeye climbed onto the roof of the village hall, in full view of everybody, and lay down flat on the slates.

He had one bullet left.

Two hundred people watched him turn the rifle away from the army. Away from the gate, away from the warlord, away from every target any of them could see — up and to the left, at the bare grey side of the mountain, where there was nothing at all.

Nineteen hundred yards. Uphill. And a crosswind pulling long white threads of snow off the ridge.

He took his hat off and set it beside him.

Then he waited, for a very long time, for the wind to stop.

---

## Part 14 — The Mountain

The wind dropped.

He fired — and for one second nobody on that wall understood what he had done. Then the sound came back down to them, because nineteen hundred yards away, up in the rocks, the bullet had hit the bell.

One note came out of it. Enormous. Low. The first note that bell had made in four months, and it climbed the mountain and kept climbing.

Nothing happened for three seconds.

Then a thin line appeared in the snow near the top, quiet, almost polite, as though somebody were drawing it with a stick. The line opened. The top of the mountain let go.

It came down the whole face at once. The noise arrived before the snow did, and nine thousand men tried to turn around in a space too narrow to turn around in.

It took eleven seconds. The pass filled to the height of a house.

Not one person in Knollen was touched.

When the white cloud thinned out, Mars was standing on the ridge above it with what was left of his army behind him. He looked down at the village for a long moment.

Then he put a fist to his chest, and took them home.

---

## Part 15 — The Song

There is a song about it now.

It gets the range wrong by about two miles. It says he fired at the warlord's heart and missed on purpose, which is backwards. It does not mention Rattletrap, or the drum, or the ram, or Snapfire, and there is no verse anywhere in it for a boy with a piece of chalk.

Sharpeye has never corrected a single word. He says the song's version is better.

The wall by the gate still says **ZERO**. Nobody has ever washed it off.

And when the mountain came down, he said nothing at all. He stood there with his hat in his hand.

It was Pim who shouted it, from the wall, with both arms in the air.

**BOOM! HEADSHOT!**

## Story structure notes

Kept here so future edits do not accidentally break the chain.

- **One goal, start to finish:** hold the pass at Knollen with eleven bullets. The reader always
  knows exactly how many are left, which is the engine of the whole story.
- **The countdown is the format.** Most parts spend one bullet and end with the new number. That
  gives every Journey level a built-in cliffhanger without inventing one, and it makes readers want
  the next part specifically to find out how he spends the next bullet. **Do not add or remove
  bullets** — the arithmetic is exact: bullet 1 (Part 4), 2 (Part 6), 3 and 4 (Part 7), 5, 6 and 7
  (Part 8), 8 (Part 9), 9 (Part 10), 10 (Part 11), and the last one fired in Part 14.
- **The climax is two parts on purpose.** Part 13 is the climb, the impossible aim at nothing, and
  the wait for the wind — it ends mid-held-breath, which is the strongest cliffhanger in the story.
  Part 14 is the shot, the bell, and the mountain coming down, and it is the longest part in the
  file. Do not compress it back into one part; the whole story is built to earn this moment.
- **Sharpeye's arc in one sentence:** a man who wants a song learns that bullets are lives, and then
  fires the one shot that nobody dies from.
- **The bragging is the arc.** He talks constantly in Parts 1–7, stops talking in Part 8, and says
  nothing at all during the famous shot. The jokes thin out at the same rate. Keep that drift; it is
  doing the character work that the story never states out loud.
- **Escalating cleverness:** Part 4 is a plain kill and he shows off. Then a drum, a lantern, a
  winch pin, an axle pin — each shot smarter and less glorious than the last, so that the final shot
  at a bell feels earned rather than random.
- **Setups and payoffs — do not remove these:**
  - **The bell and the loose snow (Part 3), which he is told about and forgets immediately** → the
    last shot (Parts 13–14). This is the single most important setup in the story. If Part 3
    changes, the ending stops working.
  - Snapfire's "the only shot anyone remembers is the last one" (Part 4) → her own famous shot was
    at a rope, and nobody sings about the rope (Part 12) → he looks up at the mountain.
  - **"BOOM! HEADSHOT!"** shouted into total silence (Part 4) → he says nothing after the real shot,
    and Pim shouts it for him (Part 15). **This is the last line of the story and the point of the
    whole thing.**
  - Pim's chalk wall (Part 2) → every count through the story → **ZERO**, never washed off (Part 15).
  - Rattletrap's six useless bullets (Part 5) → he is the one Sharpeye spends a bullet saving
    (Part 9).
  - The glory shot he announces in Part 4 is the one that bounces off Mars's shield in Part 11 — his
    public humiliation, and the low point before the last bullet.
- **Mars is never a villain.** He is honourable, he offers terms, and he salutes at the end. The
  enemy is the arithmetic, not the man.
