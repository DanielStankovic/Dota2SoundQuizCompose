# Juggernaut and the Sixty-One Carvings

A fifteen-part story about Juggernaut (Yurnero), told in simple English for a young audience.
Each part is roughly 100 words and is meant to be delivered one part per Hero Journey level
(parts 1–15 → levels 1–15).

Companion story: `lina-golden-rolling-pin.md`. Same format, same rules.

---

## Localisation notes

**Hero names are NEVER translated.** Keep them exactly as written, in every language:

- Juggernaut
- Yurnero
- Witch Doctor
- Zharvakko
- Marci
- Nerif

Other do-not-translate names:

- **Stillwater Bay** — the village. Leave as-is or transliterate. Do not translate the meaning.
- **Isle of Masks** — Yurnero's homeland. May be translated **only** if the game already has an
  official translation for it in that locale; otherwise leave as-is.
Items that *should* be translated normally:

- **"Someone call de doctor?"** — Witch Doctor's catchphrase in Part 7. Translate it into the
  target language, and if the game has an official localised version of this voice line in that
  locale, use that exact wording so players recognise it.
  Two things to preserve: it is a **question**, and it is **not** proper speech. The misspelled
  "de" instead of "the" is Zharvakko's troll accent, not a typo. Reproduce that rough, casual,
  slightly broken register however the target language does it — slang, dropped sounds, or
  non-standard spelling. If the language has no natural way to show an accent in writing, a
  plain casual version of the question is fine. Never make it sound polished or formal.

- All part titles.
- **"witch doctor"** in lowercase in Part 7 ("Zharvakko was a witch doctor") — if the locale has
  an official translation of the hero name Witch Doctor, use that wording here so the reader makes
  the connection; otherwise keep it in English.
- The names of the carved tokens (a fish, a cup, a sun, a bowl) — these are plain words, not names.
- "the oracle", "fever season" — plain words, translate naturally. Note that "the oracle" refers
  to Nerif and should match whatever the locale uses for the hero Oracle, so readers connect them.

Important: the story deliberately never names Yurnero's spell. The little wooden healing totem is
always called "the little wooden thing", "the totem" or "the ward" in lowercase, never as a spell
name, so translators do not need to match in-game spell terminology. Keep it that way.

Style guidance for translators: keep sentences short and words everyday. The original avoids
poetic or literary vocabulary on purpose, because most readers are not native English speakers.
Do not raise the register. Yurnero never speaks a single line — he must stay silent in every
language. Marci never speaks either. The only spoken line in the whole story is Witch Doctor's
catchphrase in Part 7. Keep it that way.

## Part 1 — The Man Who Came Out of the Sea

The storm broke his boat on the rocks, and the whole village watched a man walk out of the water with a sword on his back.

He wore a wooden mask. He did not take it off. He did not say one word, then or ever.

The fishermen expected trouble. The children hid behind the nets.

Only the village oracle looked pleased. Nerif was blind, and he saw things before they happened, and his visions were always true and almost never useful.

He had predicted a visitor. He had also predicted that the visitor would spend the summer doing laundry, which nobody took seriously, because the man coming up the beach looked like the end of the world.

---

## Part 2 — Stillwater Bay

Stillwater Bay is sixty-one houses on a cold island, with a mountain behind it and the Isle of Masks far across the water.

The masked man had come at the worst time of year. Every summer a fever crawls through the village, and every summer it takes a few people with it. The old doctor had died two winters ago. Nobody had replaced him.

So the village hall was full of beds. Children were coughing in the dark. The well had gone bad.

They had been sent a warrior. What they needed was a nurse.

Nobody mentioned this to him. He was very large.

---

## Part 3 — Nothing to Fight

He looked for the enemy for three days.

He walked the cliffs. He searched the caves. He stood on the beach at night and waited for something to come out of the sea. He sharpened his sword twice a day, because that was what he knew how to do.

There was no monster. There was only a fever, and a fever does not come when you call it, and it cannot be cut in half.

On the fourth morning he packed his bag. He was the best swordsman on the ocean, and here he was worth nothing at all.

---

## Part 4 — The Little Wooden Thing

On his way to the boats he passed the hall, and a small girl inside was coughing so hard she could not lie down.

He stopped. He took out a knife and a piece of driftwood, and he carved for a long time, and the villagers watched and did not understand.

Then he pushed the little wooden thing into a crack in the floor beside her bed.

It began to hum. A soft green light spread out, about four steps wide, and it was warm.

She slept the whole night without coughing once.

He unpacked his bag.

---

## Part 5 — One a Day

The totem had rules, and the rules were cruel.

It only healed inside its own little circle, about four steps across. It only hummed for a few hours. And he could only make one each day — one, no matter how long he carved, no matter how hard he tried. This was the law of his old order, and the law did not care who was sick.

So every single morning he had to choose a house.

He kept a list, scratched into a plank, and he carried it everywhere, and he did not sleep much.

---

## Part 6 — How He Talks

He never spoke, so he carved instead.

Little wooden tokens, the size of a thumb. A fish meant *the boats go out today*. A cup meant *drink more water*. A sun meant *tomorrow*. A bowl meant *eat something, you look terrible*.

He left them on doorsteps and windowsills. Soon the whole village was reading them.

The children started collecting them. A girl named Marci kept hers in a tin box under her bed.

Marci did not speak either. Not one word, ever, her whole life — which is probably why those two understood each other inside a week. By the middle of summer she had nineteen tokens, and she was the only person in Stillwater Bay who could read every single one.

---

## Part 7 — The Doctor with the Laugh

In July a doctor arrived, though not the kind anyone expected.

He came up the street shouting *Someone call de doctor?* — nobody had — and he was a troll, small and grinning, hung with feathers, beads and little bones, carrying a stick with a skull on it. Zharvakko was a witch doctor, and he said so proudly, twice.

He also laughed. At everything. At bad news, at broken bones, at your temperature. Three patients hid under their blankets the moment they heard him coming.

But Witch Doctor's medicine worked, which nobody could forgive him for.

He needed one thing to make more of it: a grey root that only grew on the far side of the island, past the rocks, where the village had stopped going years ago and did not like to explain why.

---

## Part 8 — The Mask

There was one thing the masked man could not fix.

The smallest children were frightened of him. One baby cried every time he came near, and would not stop, and the mother could only apologise.

His order did not allow him to take the mask off. Not for a meal, not for sleep, not for a crying child. That was the whole point of it.

So he started working at night instead. He carried water. He scrubbed floors. He washed and hung out the fever sheets, every night, sixty-one houses' worth.

The oracle pointed out that he had called it. Loudly. For weeks.

---

## Part 9 — The Longest Night

On the worst night of the summer, two people were dying, and they lived at opposite ends of the village, and he had one totem.

He did not choose.

He pulled it out of the floor, ran it to the other house, pushed it in beside the second bed, counted, pulled it out, and ran back. The street is four hundred steps long. He ran it forty-one times, all night, in the rain, with a sword on his back.

In the morning both of them were still breathing.

He sat down on the step and did not get up for a while.

---

## Part 10 — The Far Side of the Island

Then Zharvakko ran out of the grey root, and at last there was something the masked man was actually built for.

He went alone, over the mountain, down to the rocks the village did not talk about.

Nobody saw what happened there. It did not take long. The sound carried across the water for about a minute, and then it stopped, and the gulls went back to the cliffs.

He came back at sunset with a sack full of grey root, a limp, and a long crack straight across his mask.

He could not repair it. He wore it cracked.

---

## Part 11 — The One He Cannot Heal

Three days later he got the fever himself.

Here is the part he had never told anyone: the totem will not heal the one who made it. That is also the law of his order. He had healed sixty-one houses and could not heal himself, and he had always known it, and he had carved one every day anyway.

He hid it well. He kept washing sheets at night, slower and slower.

On the fifth evening he walked into the middle of the square, stopped, and fell over.

Sixty-one houses heard him go down. It is a very small village.

---

## Part 12 — Sixty-One People

They carried him into the hall and put him in the best bed — Marci took one end, because she is half his size and four times as strong as she looks — and then the village did for him exactly what he had been doing for them.

They took turns. Two people awake at all times, day and night, for nine days. Zharvakko fed him the medicine and laughed at him constantly, which, it turned out, is what a witch doctor does when he is worried.

Marci brought her tin box and laid the carvings out on the blanket, so he could tap the one he meant.

He mostly tapped the cup. He drank a lot of water.

---

## Part 13 — Masks

He got better. And then he was strange about it.

He kept his face turned away. He would not be looked at. The mask was cracked, he was weak, and in his old order that was a shame you carried alone.

Marci thought this was stupid, and could not tell him so, so she did something about it instead. She took a knife and a piece of driftwood, the way she had watched him do a hundred times, and carved herself a mask. It was terrible. She wore it anyway.

By the end of the week, all sixty-one houses had one.

After that he walked through Stillwater Bay in daylight, and nobody looked at him twice.

---

## Part 14 — The End of Fever Season

The fever broke in August. Nobody else died.

They held a festival in the square, because there was nothing else to do with so much relief. Somebody cooked too much fish. Zharvakko laughed for four hours. Sixty-one people danced very badly in homemade wooden masks, which from the cliffs must have looked deeply alarming.

He planted the day's totem in the middle of it all and let it hum under the dancing, for nothing, for no reason, just because he could.

Nerif was unbearable all evening. The one time an oracle gets something useful right, he makes sure nobody forgets it.

---

## Part 15 — Every Summer

His old order called him back in the autumn, the way it always does, and he went, because that is what he is.

He left in the dark, so there would be no goodbyes. But in the morning there was a carving on every single doorstep in Stillwater Bay — sixty-one of them, finished in one night, which means he had not slept at all.

On Marci's step was a small wooden figure of a man with a sword on his back and no mask on his face.

He comes back every fever season.

Her tin box does not close anymore.

## Story structure notes

Kept here so future edits do not accidentally break the chain.

- **One goal, start to finish:** keep Stillwater Bay alive through fever season. Told in order,
  no flashbacks, no mystery to hold in your head.
- **Yurnero's arc in one sentence:** the greatest swordsman alive lands somewhere his sword is
  useless, and becomes the thing the village actually needed. The heroic act in Part 9 is
  running a piece of wood back and forth in the rain, not winning a fight.
- **The format fits the hero:** Yurnero is canonically silent, so a narrated story with no
  dialogue is not a limitation here, it is the point. He communicates only by carving, which is
  what makes the ending work without a single spoken word. Marci is canonically mute too, so the
  two of them understanding each other without words is in character for both.
- **Cliffhangers that pull the reader to the next level:** he packs to leave (3), the one-a-day
  rule (5), the root beyond the rocks (7), two dying people and one totem (8→9), the cracked
  mask (10), he collapses in the square (11).
- **Setups and payoffs — do not remove these:**
  - Nerif the oracle's laundry prediction (Part 1) → he washes the fever sheets every night
    (Part 8) → Nerif is insufferable about being right (Part 14). The running joke is that his
    visions are always true and never useful.
  - "Sixty-one houses" (Part 2) → sixty-one people nursing him (Part 12) → sixty-one masks
    (Part 13) → sixty-one carvings on sixty-one doorsteps (Part 15).
  - He carves to talk, and Marci's tin box (Part 6) → she lays the tokens out so he can tap
    answers while sick (Part 12) → the tin box no longer closes (Part 15).
  - The totem will not heal its maker (Part 11) is set up by the order's rules in Part 5.
  - The mask may never come off (Part 8) → it cracks (Part 10) → he hides his face in shame
    (Part 13) → the village all wear masks so he is not the only one. **He never removes it.**
    This is canon and must stay. The payoff is the village joining him, not him unmasking.
  - The carving on Marci's doorstep (Part 15) is his own face, unmasked — the one thing he cannot
    show in person, and the closest he ever comes to speaking.
