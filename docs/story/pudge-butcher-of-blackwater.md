A fifteen-part story about Pudge, written for teenage readers. It is a boarding-school
horror-comedy: every school has a scary story, and at Greywall the scary story is a real man, and
the school has just given him a job. Dry jokes for the first half, real fear in the second, and an
ending that is meant to hit hard.

Each part is roughly 100 words and is meant to be delivered one part per Hero Journey level
(parts 1–15 → levels 1–15).

Companion stories: `lina-golden-rolling-pin.md`, `juggernaut-sixty-one-carvings.md`.

## Localisation notes

**Hero names are NEVER translated.** Keep them exactly as written, in every language:

- Pudge
- Hoodwink
- Razzil / Razzil Darkbrew (the hero Alchemist)
- Invoker
- N'aix (the hero Lifestealer)

Note: Razzil and N'aix are called by their personal names, not by their hero names, because the
story is told from inside the school and that is what the students would call them. Both are canon
Dota names. Keep them. If a locale has an official transliteration of "Razzil Darkbrew" or
"N'aix", use it.

**"the Alchemist"** — Razzil's nickname, Part 4 and the title of Part 4. This one **should** be
translated, using the locale's official wording for the hero name Alchemist, so players make the
connection. It is written in lowercase with "the" in front because it is a school nickname, not a
title. The joke behind it is explained in the next line of the part: the students think he makes
money out of nothing, and really he just bets on everything and keeps a notebook.

Other do-not-translate names:

- **Greywall** — the school. Invented place name. Leave as-is or transliterate. Do not translate
  the meaning.
- **Blackwater** — the place in the legend. Invented place name. Leave as-is or transliterate. Do
  not translate the meaning.

Items that *should* be translated, with care:

- **"the Butcher of Blackwater"** — the name of the school's scary story, and half the title.
  Translate "the Butcher of" and keep "Blackwater" untranslated. It must sound like a scary story
  that students tell each other at night, not like a job description. It appears in Parts 1, 3, 7,
  15 and the title, and must be identical in all of them.

  Second meaning, which the translator should know but must not spell out: in Part 11 we find out
  he really was a butcher at Blackwater — the man who carried the meat. If the target language has
  a word that covers both "killer" and "meat worker", use it. If it does not, choose the scary
  one, because the name comes from the children's story, not from his job.
- **"Fresh meat!"** — **Pudge's** catchphrase, Part 13. It belongs to Pudge and to nobody else;
  N'aix never speaks in this story. If the game has an official localised version of this voice
  line in that locale, use that exact wording so players recognise it. It must stay a shout.

  It carries the biggest idea in the story, so read this before translating it. In Part 11 we
  learn Pudge spent nineteen years feeding N'aix in a prison, and that he shouted **the same two
  words** down the stairs every time so the monster would come to the chain. In Part 13 he uses
  that call on purpose, at the front door, to pull N'aix away from a room full of sleeping
  students. So the line is a feeding call, not a battle cry. Part 11 says "the same two words" and
  Part 13 says "the two words he used to shout at the top of the prison stairs" — if the official
  localised voice line is not two words in that locale, change both of those phrases to match the
  real word count. They must agree.
- **"And if he waves at you, you wave back."** — the last line of the story, Part 15. This is the
  most important sentence in the file. It is a new line added to a scary story, so it should sound
  like a rule being passed down to younger students. Keep it short. Do not make it sweet and do not
  explain it.
- **"a man as big as a door"** — Parts 1 and 13. Must be the same words in both places; it is a
  callback.
- **"gardener"** — Pudge's job at the school. Use the simplest everyday word in the target
  language for the person who cuts the grass and fixes things at a school. Do not use a formal or
  technical job title.
- **"temporary"** (Part 9) — the headmaster's weasel word, shown in italics. Use the everyday word
  in the target language that someone uses when they mean "forever" but do not want to say it.
- **"all right"** (Part 9) — Pudge's only spoken line apart from the catchphrase. Keep it plain
  and short. Do not make it sound dignified or sad.
- All part titles.

Number note: nine, eleven, forty, four and two hundred come back again and again on purpose (nine
years hunting, nine new scars, eleven throws of the hook, forty jars, four club members, two
hundred students, nineteen years feeding it). Keep the numbers exactly as written.

**Swearing — intentional, keep it mild.** There are three printed swears, in Parts 5, 12 and 15.
Each one does a job: fear in the shed, fear in the dark, and a know-it-all teacher reduced to two
words. Translate them with the everyday casual equivalent in the target language — the level of
"oh crap" or "oh hell", not aggressive swearing. Do not add more, do not make them stronger, and
do not remove them.

### Style guidance — simple English is a hard rule

Most readers are **not native English speakers**, and the text will be machine-translated into
many languages. The English in this file is deliberately plain. Keep it that way when editing, and
match it when translating.

- **Short sentences.** One idea per sentence. Break long sentences in two.
- **Everyday words only.** The file says *hallway*, not corridor. *Gardener*, not groundskeeper.
  *Bed*, not cot. *Scary*, not terrifying. If a simpler word exists, it is the right word.
- **No literary or poetic language.** No long metaphors, no clever word order, no rare words.
- **Nothing the reader has to work out.** If a connection matters, the text says it directly. Part
  1 does not hint that the gardener is the man from the story — it states it.
- **Do not raise the register when translating.** Translators often make text more formal and more
  "correct" than the original. Do not. If your translation sounds like a school textbook, it is
  wrong.
- **Keep the deadpan.** The comedy and the fear both work by reporting a bad thing calmly, with
  the worst detail saved for the last line of the part. Never signal the joke and never signal the
  scare. No exclamation marks in the narration, except inside the catchphrase.
- **Almost no dialogue.** Keep it that way.

## Part 1 — The Story They Tell

Every school has a scary story. At Greywall, the story is about the Butcher of Blackwater.

The older students tell it at night, in the big room at the end of the hallway, to the new ones. A man as big as a door. A smell that reaches you before he does. A hook on a chain that comes out of the fog and takes whatever it hits. Getting hit is not the worst part.

Most school stories are not true.

This one is. The man is real, and he is alive.

This term, Greywall gave him a job as the gardener.

He started on Monday.

---

## Part 2 — The Gardener

His name is Pudge, and he waves at everybody.

That is the part nobody can deal with. He waves at students who suddenly need to read something on the wall. He waves at teachers who suddenly need to be somewhere else. He waves with his whole arm, every time. The hallway empties, and he keeps waving at the empty hallway.

He cuts the grass in straight lines that are not straight. He sleeps in the shed behind the sports field, because he asked to.

On the shed wall there is a long piece of paper with every student's name on it.

It is the scariest thing at Greywall.

---

## Part 3 — The Club

By the second week there was a club.

Four members, no permission, and a board hidden behind the boiler room. Hoodwink runs it. She is small, very fast, and has never been caught doing anything in her life. That is how you end up running a club like this.

The board has a map of where he walks, a list of who saw him and when, and a bet. Everyone put money in. The bet is about what is inside the shed. Most people said bones. The rest also said bones.

Hoodwink has read the Butcher of Blackwater story twice.

The hands match.

---

## Part 4 — The Alchemist

Razzil Darkbrew is in the year below. Everyone calls him the Alchemist, because he makes money out of nothing.

He does not make it out of nothing. He makes it by betting. He will bet on anything: the weather, a test score, how long a lesson runs over. He keeps a notebook. He almost never loses.

He is not in the club, but he found their bet, and he put his own money on top of it, because a small bet is no fun to win.

Then it got slow. So he turned it into a dare. Go in the shed, bring one thing out, take all of it.

Hoodwink said no twice. Then she spent the afternoon working out which window.

On Friday night, four of them crossed the sports field with one torch.

---

## Part 5 — What Is In The Shed

There are no bones.

There is a bed with the end cut off, because he does not fit. A pot on a small stove. Forty jars of something orange, and someone has written a label on each one, slowly and carefully. One chair. A radio that only finds one station.

And the list on the wall. Every name in the school, with a small mark next to some of them.

Nobody had time to work out what the marks meant, because Hoodwink turned round with the torch.

On the back wall, on two nails, hung a hook on a chain.

Somebody whispered: *oh, fuck.*

---

## Part 6 — He Knew

They went out through the window and broke it.

He knew. He was standing fifty steps away in the dark the whole time. He had heard them cross the field, because they crossed it like a herd of cows, and he decided to let them finish.

He did not tell anyone. He fixed the window himself on Sunday.

Then he cut the grass, and waved, and for four days nothing happened. The waiting was worse than being caught.

On Thursday, all four of them found a jar of the orange stuff outside their door.

It was jam. It was very good jam.

---

## Part 7 — Invoker Is Never Wrong

Invoker teaches at Greywall, and he will tell you that.

He talks in very long sentences. He finishes other people's sentences for them. He was right about one thing in a staff meeting two years ago and has brought it up eleven times since. Older students copy the way he talks.

He looked at the new gardener once. Then he did the thing the students could not do: he read the school papers.

No documents. No family name. No home town. One letter about him, signed by a man who does not exist.

So Invoker wrote to the city and asked about the Butcher of Blackwater.

---

## Part 8 — The Woods

It started with animals.

A fox on the path, with very little left of it. Then the school chickens. All of them, in one night. The wire was not cut. It was pulled open from the inside, and the door was still locked.

Then the sports shed, which is a different shed, was torn open like wet paper.

The woods behind Greywall go back for miles. Nobody ever cared about them before. Now two hundred students watch them through the classroom windows instead of working.

Two hundred students, and every one of them knew who to blame.

The man owns a hook.

---

## Part 9 — Temporary

The club's board stopped being funny.

The bet came down. The map stayed up, and somebody drew a new red line on it, from the woods to the shed. Parents wrote letters. People from the town came up the hill in the daytime, which they had not done for years.

The headmaster is not a brave man. On Friday he told Pudge to go. He said the word *temporary* four times, and he did not look at him once.

Pudge said one thing. He said *all right*.

Then he went outside and finished the line of grass he had started.

---

## Part 10 — Tuesday

He left on a Tuesday, with one bag, and the bag was not full.

He gave the jam away first. All forty jars, to the kitchen, with a note about how long it keeps. He gave back the shed key. He stood at the gate for a while and looked up at the building. Then he waved at it.

Every window was full of people. Nobody waved back.

Only Hoodwink did, from the boiler room roof, where she is not allowed to be.

He walked down the hill.

That night, the lights at Greywall went out at ten past one.

---

## Part 11 — Blackwater

Blackwater was not a town. It was a prison built to hold N'aix.

They kept it in a pit, and somebody had to feed it. For nineteen years that was Pudge. He carried the meat down the stairs twice a day, and he shouted the same two words first so the thing would come to its chain.

Nine years ago the chains came apart. Nobody at Blackwater lived except him.

He has followed it ever since. In the spring it stopped in the woods behind Greywall.

He did not come here for a job. He took the job because the shed is nearest to the woods.

He never knew why it had stopped here. It had stopped because there were two hundred children inside.

---

## Part 12 — Ten Past One

The lights went out at ten past one, and they did not come back on.

Two hundred students. One building. Nobody awake.

N'aix came in through the kitchen door, and it was in no hurry. It opened doors on the way. There was no lock in that school it could not pull apart, and the sound it made while it worked on a lock was worse than anything that came after.

Razzil was under a bed with both hands over his own mouth, saying *oh fuck, oh fuck.*

Then it reached the big room at the end of the hallway. The room where they tell the story.

---

## Part 13 — Fresh Meat

Pudge was six miles down the road when he looked back.

Greywall stands on a hill. You can see it from the road, and there are always lights in the windows. That night there were none.

He had been sent away. Nobody had asked him to come back. He turned round anyway, and it took him two hours, uphill, in the dark.

A man as big as a door reached the front steps at three in the morning. He stood there and listened to what was happening inside.

Then he shouted the two words he used to shout at the top of the prison stairs.

*Fresh meat!*

---

## Part 14 — The Hook

N'aix came. It always comes when it hears that.

Pudge fought it in the hallway, alone, between N'aix and the stairs, for three hours.

The story the students tell is wrong about one thing. The hook is not the scary part. The hook is the part that saves you.

He threw it eleven times that night. Eleven times it came back with N'aix on the end of the chain.

It finished when the sun came up, and N'aix did not get up again.

Pudge won, and only just. He has nine new scars and his left arm does not lift above his shoulder any more.

Razzil brought him water. Hoodwink would not leave him.

---

## Part 15 — The List

Invoker's answer from the city arrived that same morning. It said the Butcher of Blackwater is only a story, and not a real man. Invoker read it twice, then said: *oh, fuck off.*

Later somebody finally asked about the marks on the list.

They were birthdays. He had written down every student in the school, and all term he had been working out whose birthday was coming next. Nobody had ever done that for him.

He still works at Greywall. The jam is back on the breakfast table.

And they still tell the story at night, to the new ones. It has one more line at the end now.

*And if he waves at you, you wave back.*

## Story structure notes

Kept here so future edits do not accidentally break the chain.

- **Audience:** teenagers, not small children. That is why the setting is a school, the cast are
  students, and the second half is genuinely frightening. Do not soften Parts 8, 11 or 12.
- **Part 1 must stay explicit.** It states four things in order: the school has a scary story, the
  story is about the Butcher of Blackwater, the story is true and the man is alive, and that man is
  now the school gardener. An earlier draft only implied the last step and readers missed it. Never
  turn this back into a hint.
- **One goal, start to finish:** the school finds out what the gardener is. The reader is told the
  answer in Part 1, so the tension comes from the reader being ahead of the characters, not from a
  mystery. Nothing is held back as a puzzle.
- **Pudge's arc in one sentence:** the monster everyone is scared of is the only one who comes
  back. The brave act in Part 12 is walking four hours back up a hill to a school that threw him
  out.
- **Tone turn:** Parts 1–7 are dry school comedy with a cold edge. Part 8 is where the comedy
  stops. Parts 9–10 are the low point. Parts 11–14 are horror and payoff. Part 15 lands the heart.
- **Parts 11–14 must never be confusing about who is who.** An earlier draft kept the monster
  unnamed until after the rescue, so readers could not tell the monster and Pudge apart and
  thought the catchphrase belonged to the monster. The fixed order is strict and must be kept:
  **11 explains, 12 is the monster only, 13 is Pudge only, 14 is the fight.** One character per
  part until they meet.
- **Part 11 is the keystone and must stay exactly where it is.** It answers every question the
  story has raised, in plain order: what Blackwater was (a prison built to hold N'aix), who Pudge
  was there (the man who fed it, for nineteen years), why he hunts it (nine years ago it got out
  and killed everyone at Blackwater except him), where it went (the woods behind the school, in
  the spring), **why he is working as a school gardener at all** (he did not come for the job — he
  took it because the shed is the nearest building to the woods), and why N'aix stopped here (two
  hundred children). Earlier drafts were missing the gardener line, and without it the whole
  premise falls apart: a man hunting a monster does not take a job cutting grass. Do not shorten
  this part and do not move any of it into the fight.
- **The timeline, so edits stay consistent:** nineteen years feeding N'aix at Blackwater → nine
  years ago it escapes and Blackwater dies → nine years of following it → in the spring it stops
  in the woods behind Greywall → he takes the gardener's job → the story begins this term.
- **Early parts stand alone:** Parts 1–7 each end on their own joke or scare, because levels 1–6
  are where readers drop off. Part 1 ends on the best hook in the story and must stay the opener.
- **Cliffhangers that pull the reader to the next level:** he started on Monday (1), the list (2),
  the hands match (3), four students crossing the field (4), the hook on the wall (5), four silent
  days (6), the letter to the city (7), everyone blames him (8), he is sent away (9), the lights
  go out (10), it stopped here because of the children (11), N'aix reaches the room where they
  sleep (12), he shouts the feeding call (13).
- **Setups and payoffs — do not remove these:**
  - He waves and nobody waves back (Part 2) → every window is full of people and nobody waves
    (Part 10) → the story's new last line tells you to wave back (Part 15). **This is the ending of
    the whole story. Everything else exists to set up that last line.**
  - The list of every student's name, with marks (Parts 2 and 5) → the marks are birthdays, and
    why (Part 15).
  - "A man as big as a door" (Part 1) → the same words describe him arriving to save them
    (Part 13).
  - **The feeding call.** He shouted two words down the prison stairs for nineteen years so the
    monster would come to him (Part 11) → he shouts the same two words at the front door of the
    school so the monster comes to him and not to the students (Part 13) → "it always comes when
    it hears that" (Part 14). This is why the catchphrase is in the story at all. Never turn it
    into a battle cry.
  - **The word "butcher".** It is a scary story name for eleven parts (Parts 1, 3, 7) → in Part 11
    we learn he was literally the butcher, the man who carried the meat → Invoker gets a letter
    saying the Butcher of Blackwater does not exist, on the morning after he saved the school
    (Part 15).
  - The forty jars of orange stuff (Part 5) → it is jam, left outside the thieves' doors (Part 6)
    → he gives all forty to the kitchen when he leaves (Part 10) → it is on the breakfast table
    every morning (Part 15).
  - The hook on the shed wall scares them (Part 5) → "the man owns a hook" is why they all blame
    him (Part 8) → the hook is the thing that saves two hundred people (Part 14). The line "the
    hook is not the scary part, the hook is the part that saves you" is the turn of the whole
    story and must stay in Part 14.
  - The woods behind Greywall, where the animals are being killed (Part 8) → that is where N'aix
    stopped walking, and why (Part 11). Part 8 is the only setup N'aix gets before Part 11, so it
    must stay.
  - The big room at the end of the hallway, where the story is told (Part 1) → it is the room
    N'aix reaches (Part 12) → it is where the new version of the story is told (Part 15).
  - He counts lights in windows from the road (Part 13), which is only possible because he spent
    all term outside looking at that building. He comes back because the school is dark, not
    because anyone called him. Nobody ever asks him to come back.
  - He sleeps in the shed behind the sports field "because he asked to" (Part 2) → the shed is the
    nearest building to the woods (Part 11). He was never a gardener. He was standing guard for
    the whole first half of the story and nobody noticed.
  - Razzil bets on everything and almost never loses (Part 4) → his money starts the dare that
    gets four students into the shed, and after that it buys nothing that matters; the only
    useful thing he ever does is carry a cup of water (Part 14). Keep that contrast.
  - Invoker reads the school papers and finds nothing (Part 7) → his answer from the city says the
    Butcher does not exist, and arrives the morning after the Butcher saved the school (Part 14).
    The joke is that the man who is never wrong gets written proof that he is wrong.
  - Hoodwink breaks in first (Parts 4–5), is the only one who waves goodbye (Part 10), and will not
    leave him afterwards (Part 14). Her arc is the student version of the same idea.
  - Blackwater is only a scary word until Part 11, where it becomes a real place with a real reason
    to be feared.
- **The sincere beats are Part 10, the end of Part 14, and most of Part 15, and they must stay
  sincere.** Part 15 gets one joke at the top — Invoker's letter, which is the last laugh in the
  story — and after that nothing is funny again. No jokes in the gate scene or the birthday
  reveal. The horror only matters because the reader has started to like him, and the comedy of
  the first half only pays off if the ending hurts.
- **Never explain the ending.** Part 15 states the facts and stops. Do not add a line telling the
  reader how to feel about the wave.
