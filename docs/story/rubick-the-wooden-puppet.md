# Rubick the Wooden Puppet

A fifteen-part story about Rubick, told in simple English for a young audience. It is an **origin
story**: a wooden puppet is struck by green lightning, names himself by accident, and learns the
world by copying everyone he sees — then doing it better. By the last part the reader has watched
him become the Grand Magus they play in the game.

Each part is roughly 100 words and is meant to be delivered one part per Hero Journey level
(parts 1–15 → levels 1–15).

Companion stories: `lina-golden-rolling-pin.md`, `juggernaut-sixty-one-carvings.md`,
`pudge-butcher-of-blackwater.md`, `sniper-eleven-bullets.md`,
`tinker-the-cupboard-kids.md`, `invoker-three-wisps.md`.

Note for future agents: Rubick also appears as a **side character** in
`tinker-the-cupboard-kids.md` (a schoolboy who lifts lunch trays) and in the archived
`docs/story/archive/pudge-big-papa-meat.md` (an office worker who quietly fixes spreadsheets).
Those are different stories in different worlds and they share nothing with this one. This file
is the only story where Rubick is the hero. Do not merge them, do not carry anything from one
into the other, and do not ship the archive draft.

Meepo and Windranger also appear in `tinker-the-cupboard-kids.md`. Same rule: different world,
different people, no crossover.

Note on sources: Liquipedia blocks the fetch tool with a human-verification page, so the hero
details here come from known Dota 2 canon (Rubick's lift and his habit of stealing a spell and
casting it better, Pangolier's sword strike and roll, Windranger's long shot, Meepo's identical
copies), not from a live lore read.

## Localisation notes

**Hero names are NEVER translated.** Keep them exactly as written, in every language:

- Rubick
- Pango (the hero Pangolier)
- Windranger
- Meepo / the Meepos

Notes on these four:

- **Rubick** is never translated and never replaced with a local name, including in Part 4 where
  the puppet invents the name by mishearing two English words. See the pun note below.
- **Pango** is the short name players already use for Pangolier. Keep "Pango". Do not expand it
  to Pangolier in the body text, and do not replace it with his canon personal name. If a locale
  has an official transliteration of "Pango", use it.
- **Windranger** is used as a public nickname here, not as a surname. Her real name stays a
  secret on purpose (in `tinker-the-cupboard-kids.md` she is called Lyralei; that is a different
  story — do not import the name). Keep "Windranger". If the game has an official translation of
  the hero name Windranger in that locale, **use that official string**, because the whole point
  of Part 9 is that the reader recognises her.
- **Meepo** is never translated. **the Meepos** (Parts 11–13) is just the plural of that name,
  because five of him look identical. Keep the hero name and add the locale's normal plural
  ending, or repeat the name with a number if the language has no clean plural. Do not translate
  it into "the copies" or "the twins".

Other do-not-translate names:

- None. This story has no invented town, shop, or company. It is "a small, quiet town" on
  purpose.

Items that *should* be translated, with care:

- **"the Grand Magus"** (Part 15, and the Part 15 title). This is Rubick's official in-game
  title, and the whole point of the last part. **Use the official in-game localised version of
  Grand Magus**, exactly, so the player recognises it. It appears twice in Part 15: first in the
  elder's spoken line as *a grand magus* (lowercase, as if he is describing a kind of person),
  then as **the Grand Magus** once it has become a name. Keep that step-up. Both must still be
  recognisably the official title — change the sentence around the name if you have to, never
  the name.
- **"quick strike"** (Parts 7, 8, 14) and **"fast, spinning roll" / "fast roll"** (Parts 7, 8).
  These are Pangolier's real abilities, hidden in plain sight: **Swashbuckle** and **Rolling
  Thunder**. In English they stay as ordinary description. When translating, **use the official
  in-game localised names** if they fit as plain words inside the sentence. If the official
  names would sound like labels stuck onto a children's story, translate the meaning (a very
  fast sword poke; a spinning roll across the ground) and keep the same wording every time each
  one appears.
- **"longshot" / "longshot bow technique"** (Parts 9, 10, 14). This is Windranger's **Powershot**.
  Same rule as above: use the official in-game name if it reads as a technique a person would
  actually say out loud; otherwise translate "long shot" as a powerful shot from far away, and
  keep the wording identical in all three parts.
- **"holding it up with his mind"** (Part 6). This is Rubick's **Telekinesis**. Do not insert
  the ability name into the English. In translation, keep it as a mind-lift — a wooden boy
  holding a tree in the air without touching it. Do not turn it into a spell chant.
- **"copied" / "copying"** (Parts 5, 8, 10, 14, 15). This is the whole story, and it is Spell
  Steal told as a physical habit. Use the everyday verb for copying a movement by watching
  someone. Do not use a magic word, and do not write "steal" — in this telling he is a child
  learning, not a thief.
- **"hide and seek"** (Part 13, and the Part 13 title). Use the locale's normal name for that
  children's game.
- All part titles.

### The name pun in Part 4 — keep the English, explain the sound

This is the single most important instruction in this file. Do not try to invent a new pun
in the target language. It will not work, because the name at the end has to stay **Rubick**.

Leave these three lines in English, in Latin letters, in every locale — including languages
that do not use the Latin alphabet:

- **"Rub the brick! Rub the brick clean!"**
- **"Rub... brick. Rub... ick."**
- **rub**, **brick**, and **Rubick** in the explanation sentence

This is the same rule as "Ogre Magi" / "Maggs" in `lina-golden-rolling-pin.md`: the joke is
the English sound, so the English has to stay on the page.

Translate everything around those lines. The surrounding narration is how the reader
understands the joke:

1. The puppet **does not know what the words mean**. Keep that. In translation it is even
   more true, because the shout is now in a language the reader may not speak either.
2. He **only liked the sound**.
3. **"Said slowly, rub and brick sounded like a name: Rubick."** Translate this sentence
   fully, but leave *rub*, *brick* and *Rubick* in English. This is the explanation. Do not
   drop it, and do not replace it with a footnote or a translator's note. The story has to
   teach the sound by itself.

Do not rewrite the builders' shout into the target language. Do not add brackets, phonetic
spellings, or a line that says "this is an English pun". The broken practice line must stay
broken. The pauses are his wooden jaw, not drama. Keep the dots.

### Style guidance — simple English is a hard rule

Most readers are **not native English speakers**, and the text will be machine-translated into
many languages. The English in this file is deliberately plain. Keep it that way when editing,
and match it when translating.

- **Short sentences.** One idea per sentence. Break long sentences in two.
- **Everyday words only.** The file says *boy*, not youth. *Copy*, not imitate. *Frightened*,
  not terrified. If a simpler word exists, it is the right word.
- **No literary or poetic language.** No long metaphors, no clever word order, no rare words.
- **Do not raise the register when translating.** If your translation sounds like a school
  textbook, it is wrong.
- **This story uses a little dialogue, and that is allowed.** The other stories in this folder
  are almost entirely narrated. Here the dialogue is short and rare: the toy maker's whisper,
  the builders' shout, the woman's question, the elder's two sentences. Keep those. Do not
  convert them to narration and do not add more.
- **Green stays green.** The storm, the lightning and the light in the puppet's chest are green
  in every language. That is Rubick's colour in the game. Do not change it to white, gold, or
  "magic-coloured".

**Swearing — there is none in this story, on purpose.** This is the young-audience end of the
folder. Do not add any, in any language.

## Part 1 — The Puppet in the Window

In a small, quiet town, an old toy maker built a special puppet. He used dark wood and thick strings. He wanted the puppet to look like a young boy. The puppet sat in the window for months. It could not move or speak. It was just an object. People passed by the shop, looking at its blank wooden face. The old man was sad because he had no children. He often talked to the puppet as if it were a real boy. "One day, you will walk," he whispered. But the puppet remained still, waiting in the dark.

---

## Part 2 — Green Lightning

One night, a strange storm covered the town. The sky turned bright green instead of dark black. Thunder shook the small toy shop. A wild flash of green lightning struck the roof. The energy traveled down the chimney and hit the wooden puppet in the window. The puppet shook violently. The thick strings burned away, but the wood did not catch fire. A soft green light glowed inside its wooden chest. Slowly, the wooden eyes opened. The puppet looked around the room. The strange storm outside stopped, but a new life had just started inside the shop.

---

## Part 3 — Forever a Puppet

The puppet tried to stand, but his body was clumsy. He was made of hard wood and loose joints. When he took a step, his legs moved in sharp, stiff motions. He fell flat on the floor with a loud wooden sound. He did not feel pain, only confusion. He looked at his hands and tried to bend his fingers. It was hard. He realized he would never have the smooth, natural motion of real people. He had to swing his arms and click his joints to move forward. He was a living boy, but forever a puppet.

---

## Part 4 — Rubick

The puppet wandered out of the shop into the morning market. He did not know what he was. He did not even have a name. He hid behind a cart and listened to two builders fixing a wall. One yelled, "Rub the brick! Rub the brick clean!" The puppet did not know what the words meant. He only liked the sound. He practiced them with his stiff mouth. "Rub... brick. Rub... ick." Said slowly, rub and brick sounded like a name: Rubick. When a woman later asked who he was, he smiled and said, "Rubick."

---

## Part 5 — Copying

Rubick stayed in the town and lived like a young boy. He was different from normal kids, and everyone could see it. His wooden face and jerky movements made him stand out. Because his mind was fresh, he did not know anything about the world. To survive, he began to copy other people. If he saw a baker knead dough, Rubick copied the exact hand movements. If a boy waved, Rubick waved the exact same way. Copying became his way of learning. He watched closely, turning every new sight into a lesson for his wooden body.

---

## Part 6 — The Floating Tree

One day, Rubick was walking near the woods. A group of men were cutting down a very large tree. Suddenly, the thick ropes broke. The heavy tree started falling right toward Rubick. He was too slow to run away with his stiff legs. In a panic, he put his wooden hands up and closed his eyes. But the crash never came. Rubick opened his eyes and saw the giant tree floating in the air. He was holding it up with his mind! He gently moved his hands, and the tree landed safely. This strange power frightened him.

---

## Part 7 — Pango

Rubick loved to visit the town square to watch travelers. One afternoon, a French musketeer named Pango came to visit. Pango was famous for his amazing sword skills. Rubick watched closely as Pango performed a show. Pango used a very quick strike with his pointy sword. Then, he did a fast, spinning roll on the ground to move away. Rubick stared without blinking, not even once. He memorized the exact angle of Pango's arm and the speed of his roll. Rubick did not have a sword, but he picked up a stick to practice those moves.

---

## Part 8 — Better Than Pango

Later that day, Rubick saw a heavy apple cart rolling down a steep hill. It was going to crash into a brick wall. Rubick remembered Pango's movements. He used his stick to execute a quick strike to block the wheel. Then, he did a fast roll over the cart to push it to a safe stop. As he finished the roll, Rubick noticed something amazing. His roll was faster and smoother than Pango's roll. Even though he was a stiff wooden puppet, the movements he copied became even sharper and better than the original person.

---

## Part 9 — Windranger

Next, a famous traveler came to town. She was a top-class archer, the best anyone in town had seen. Her real name was a secret, but everyone called her Windranger. She was known for her powerful longshot bow technique. Rubick watched her practice in the fields. She pulled the heavy bowstring back, lined up her body perfectly, and released the arrow with tremendous force. The arrow flew straight through three thick wooden targets at once. Rubick's wooden eyes watched her feet, her hands, and her posture. He memorized every detail of her longshot technique, eager to try it himself.

---

## Part 10 — Better Than the Teacher

The next morning, Rubick carved a simple wooden bow and arrow. He wanted to practice what he saw. He stood in Windranger's exact stance in the open field. Rubick pulled the bowstring back, adjusting his elbows, shoulders, and posture just like she did. When he released the arrow, it flew through the air with incredible speed and power. The arrow flew far beyond Windranger's original distance, striking a small target on a distant tree. Rubick smiled. He realized that whenever he copied a person's technique, his wooden body somehow performed the movement even better than the original teacher.

---

## Part 11 — The Meepos

Rubick liked to explore the edges of the town. One morning, he passed by the local prison yard. He saw a very strange sight. There were five prisoners working in the dirt. The most interesting thing was that all five prisoners looked exactly the same. No one could tell them apart. Because of this, the guards just called them the Meepos. The Meepos were using shovels to dig a deep trench. They moved with incredible speed and rhythm, digging into the earth in perfect harmony. Rubick watched from behind the fence, his mind recording their every move.

---

## Part 12 — Underground

Rubick wanted to learn the Meepos' digging technique. The Meepos did not just dig with shovels; they used quick body turns and coordinated hand sweeps to clear dirt instantly. Rubick stood in the woods and practiced the exact same movements. He rotated his stiff wooden body and scooped the dirt with his hands. To his surprise, his technique worked perfectly. It was even faster than the Meepos. He could clear dirt so fast that he sank down into the ground in seconds. He covered his head with loose dirt, completely hidden, before popping back up with a grin.

---

## Part 13 — Hide and Seek

That afternoon, Rubick played hide and seek with the other village children. They usually found him quickly because of his loud, wooden footsteps. But this time was different. When it was time to hide, Rubick ran to the garden and used the Meepos' fast digging technique. He moved his hands rapidly, burrowed underground, and covered himself with dirt. The children searched everywhere. They looked in the trees and behind the houses. Hours passed, and the sun went down. The other kids finally went home. Rubick stayed underground for a whole day, perfectly hidden and victorious.

---

## Part 14 — Any Skill

As the years passed, Rubick showed more of his copied techniques. If he saw a builder work fast, Rubick built faster. If he saw a worker chop heavy wood, Rubick chopped with flawless precision. He used Pango's quick strike to trim high branches and Windranger's longshot bow technique to knock down apples from distant trees. The townspeople were amazed. They saw a wooden puppet who could master any physical skill instantly. No ordinary person could do so many different things so well. People watched him work and wondered how a wooden boy could be so talented.

---

## Part 15 — The Grand Magus

The stories about the puppet boy spread to neighboring lands. People stopped calling him just a wooden boy. Because he mastered every physical movement he saw and performed it even better than his teachers, the townsfolk were in awe. "No normal human can learn all of this," the town elder said one day. "His skill feels like magic. He is a grand magus!" The name stuck. Rubick, the wooden puppet, became known to everyone as the Grand Magus. He still walked with stiff, jerky steps. But inside his wooden body, he held the power and secrets of the whole world, always ready to learn more.

## Story structure notes

Kept here so future edits do not accidentally break the chain.

- **Audience:** the young end of the folder. Closest in tone to `invoker-three-wisps.md`,
  `lina-golden-rolling-pin.md` and `juggernaut-sixty-one-carvings.md`. No swearing, no real
  horror, nobody dies.
- **One goal, stated early:** Part 1 is a puppet who cannot walk. Part 15 is a magus who can
  copy the whole world. The reader is watching him become someone, not watching him solve a
  quest.
- **Rubick's arc in one sentence:** a wooden boy with no name and no smooth movement learns
  every skill by copying it, and the copying itself turns out to be the magic.
- **This is an origin story, and the reveal is for the player, not for Rubick.** He is called
  Rubick from Part 4, but he is never called a hero inside the story. The reader works it out
  from the green light, the mind-lift, the copies that come out better than the original, and
  the title Grand Magus. Do not add a line that says he became the Dota hero.
- **Shape:** Parts 1–3 are the lifeless puppet becoming a clumsy living boy. Part 4 gives him
  a name. Part 5 states the method (copy). Part 6 is the first magic (the lift). Parts 7–13 are
  three stolen lessons, each taught then immediately used, each coming out better: Pango
  (7–8), Windranger (9–10), the Meepos (11–13). Part 14 shows years of the same trick. Part 15
  names him.
- **The three-lesson pattern must stay.** Each visitor is watched, memorised, then used in the
  next part, and the copy is better than the teacher. Three times, same beat, bigger each time.
  Part 8 is a cart. Part 10 is a shot across a field. Part 13 is a whole day underground. Do
  not flatten this into "he copied many people".
- **Setups and payoffs — do not remove these:**
  - **"One day, you will walk"** (Part 1) → he does walk, but **"forever a puppet"** (Part 3)
    → **"He still walked with stiff, jerky steps"** (Part 15). The body never becomes human.
    The ending only works if he is still wooden.
  - **The strings burn away, the wood does not catch fire, green light in the chest** (Part 2).
    That is the only origin he gets. Do not add a second one. Green is not decoration — it is
    how a player recognises him.
  - **Stiff legs, too slow to run** (Parts 3, 6) → the copied roll is **"faster and smoother
    than Pango's"** (Part 8). The joke is that the worst body in town makes the best copy.
  - **"Copying became his way of learning"** (Part 5) is the thesis. Parts 8, 10 and 15 say it
    again, louder: the copy is **better than the original**. That is Spell Steal, told without
    the ability name. Do not weaken "better" to "just as good".
  - **Pango's quick strike and spinning roll** (Part 7) → save the apple cart (Part 8) → trim
    high branches (Part 14). Both moves come back. Keep both.
  - **Windranger's longshot** (Part 9) → farther than hers (Part 10) → knocks down apples
    (Part 14). Same chain.
  - **Five identical Meepos digging in rhythm** (Part 11) → **"even faster than the Meepos"**
    (Part 12) → hide and seek, a whole day underground (Part 13). The loud wooden footsteps
    in Part 13 only pay off if he has always been easy to find. The Meepo lesson must keep
    the same "better than the teacher" beat as Pango and Windranger.
  - **No name** (Part 4) → he copies a sound he does not understand, **"rub and brick
    sounded like a name: Rubick"** (Part 4) → **a wooden boy** the town still cannot explain
    (Part 14) → **the Grand Magus** (Part 15). The last name is given by other people. The
    first one is a sound he stole. He never names himself a second time. The English shout
    in Part 4 is load-bearing; do not translate it and do not replace the explanation
    sentence with a cleverer joke.
- **Canon notes for future edits:**
  - Rubick lifts with his mind (Telekinesis) and copies what he sees, then casts it better
    (Spell Steal). Never swap those two. The lift is Part 6 only; the copying is the rest of
    the story.
  - Pango is Pangolier. The quick strike is Swashbuckle. The spinning roll is Rolling Thunder.
    He is written as a French musketeer because that is how the hero plays; do not turn him
    into an animal on the page.
  - Windranger's longshot is Powershot, including the shot that goes through more than one
    target (Part 9, three wooden targets). Keep the three targets.
  - The five identical Meepos are Divided We Stand. The digging is this story's invention —
    Meepo has no burrow spell. It is allowed to stay as a copied work technique, but do not
    add a Dota ability name on top of it.
  - A wooden puppet origin is this story's invention, not Dota canon. It is a children's
    telling and it is allowed to stay, but do not add lore that contradicts the game more
    deeply than this already does (do not give him a human family, a real childhood, or a
    scene where the wood turns to skin).
- **Never explain the ending.** Part 15 states the new name and stops. Do not add a line
  telling the reader that Rubick became the hero they play.
